# Graph-literacy levels

Use this six-level guide to choose charts for a reader. Aim for Levels 1-2 in the main view. Keep harder charts when the question needs them. Lead with a simple finding, then show the full evidence.

> **Source and limit:** These levels come from the user-supplied image, "Which graphs can people read?" (shared 2026-11-21). Treat them as a design guide, not a tested scale of what a person can read. The image cites OECD PIAAC 2023, but it does not show that OECD tested this chart-level scale. Check with real readers when the stakes are high.

| Level | Reader described in the image | Example forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1: Newcomer** | No math or science background. Needs the takeaway, not the data. | Big number, icon array, up/down arrow. |
| **2: Everyday reader** | Reads news and app charts, such as fitness or finance. | Before/after bars, single-% ring, ranked bars. |
| **3: Informed reader** | Has high-school or intro-college statistics. Follows trends over time. | Line over time, grouped bars, 100% stacked bar. |
| **4: Science-literate** | Has college science training. Includes nurses, coaches, and health-tech readers. | Bars with error bars, scatter with trend, box plot. |
| **5: Practitioner** | Includes clinicians, analysts, and researchers outside the field. | Distribution, forest plot, heatmap. |
| **6: Specialist (PhD)** | Includes omics and biostatistics experts. Reads raw model output. | Volcano plot, PCA clusters, network graph. |

The levels build on each other. A reader at Level 6 can also read simpler charts. The chart examples show likely familiarity, not a fixed rule about a person's skill. Choose a chart for the question and test it with the audience.

## Aim for Levels 1-2

- Put the finding in plain words. Give each big number a unit, time span, source, and fair point of comparison.
- Use ranked or before/after bars for clear differences. Label bars and use a common, honest scale.
- Give icon arrays a clear denominator. Label arrow size as well as direction.
- Show the number on a single-share ring. Use text or bars if readers need exact comparisons.
- Keep a Level 3-6 chart if it shows a needed trend, range, or model result. Pair it with a Level 1-2 takeaway. Keep uncertainty and key limits visible.
- Use text labels and more than color to carry meaning.

**Check:** Can a labeled number or a short bar chart tell the truth? If not, use the next level needed and lead with a simple summary.

## Glossary

- **PCA:** Principal component analysis. A way to show how groups of data points vary.
- **Omics:** Large-scale study of genes, proteins, or similar biological data.
- **Forest plot:** A chart of estimates and their ranges from several studies or groups.
- **Volcano plot:** A chart that compares effect size with statistical strength.
